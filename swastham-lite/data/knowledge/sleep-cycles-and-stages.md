# Sleep Cycles and Stages

A typical night of sleep is organized into repeating cycles, each lasting roughly 90 minutes. Within each cycle, the body moves through several stages: light non-REM sleep, deep non-REM sleep, and REM (rapid eye movement) sleep. Most adults go through four to six such cycles per night.

Deep non-REM sleep, concentrated in the earlier cycles of the night, is associated with physical restoration, tissue repair, and immune function. REM sleep, which becomes more prominent in later cycles closer to waking, is associated with memory consolidation, emotional processing, and dreaming.

Because deep sleep is concentrated earlier in the night, cutting sleep short by staying up late tends to reduce deep sleep more than REM sleep. Conversely, waking up too early can disproportionately cut into REM-heavy later cycles.

Understanding sleep architecture explains why both total sleep duration and sleep timing matter, not just the number of hours logged. A fragmented night with frequent awakenings can reduce time spent in restorative deep sleep even if total time in bed appears adequate.
